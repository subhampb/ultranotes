## Electrostatic Potential

$$\mathbf{E} = -\nabla \phi \quad \text{from } \nabla \times \mathbf{E} = 0$$

$$\phi(\mathbf{x}) \to 0 \quad \text{as } \vert{}\mathbf{x}\vert{} \to \infty$$

### Conserved Energy

$$E = \frac{1}{2} m \dot{\mathbf{x}} \cdot \dot{\mathbf{x}} + q \phi(\mathbf{x})$$

### Poisson and Laplace Equations

Gauss's law becomes:

$$\nabla \cdot \mathbf{E} = \frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0} \implies \nabla^2 \phi = -\frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0} \quad \text{(Poisson equation)}$$

If charge density vanishes ($\rho = 0$):

$$\nabla^2 \phi = 0$$

Solutions of this equation are called **harmonic functions**. This equation is called the **Laplace equation**.

$\nabla^2 \phi = 0$ is linear. So, if $\phi_1(\mathbf{x})$ and $\phi_2(\mathbf{x})$ are solutions, then $\phi_1(\mathbf{x}) + \phi_2(\mathbf{x})$ is a solution too.

So, if $\phi_1$ is the potential for charge distribution $\rho_1$, and $\phi_2$ is the potential for charge distribution $\rho_2$, then the potential for $\rho_1 + \rho_2$ is just $\phi_1 + \phi_2$.

This linearity tells us the electric field of a collection of charges is just the sum of the fields generated by each charge. This is called the **principle of superposition**.
